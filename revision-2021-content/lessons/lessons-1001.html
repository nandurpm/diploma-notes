1. Listening/Reading and comprehending a technology-related inspirational speech or story

Concept

Comprehension means constructing meaning from spoken or written language. In a technology-related speech or story, the learner should identify the central message, the situation, the speaker’s purpose, important facts, sequence, and supporting details. Listening and reading use different physical channels, but both require attention, prediction, selection of evidence, and checking of meaning.

Method

  1. Preview the title and predict the subject.
  2. Listen or read once for the main idea without stopping for every unfamiliar word.
  3. Read or listen again and record the five questions: who, what, when, where, and why.
  4. Separate the central message from examples, illustrations, and repeated phrases.
  5. Answer in complete sentences and support answers with details from the passage.
Illustrative practice passage: “A low-cost sensor was installed in a workshop to measure temperature continuously. The system warned the operator before the motor overheated.”
Required: State the main idea and one supporting detail.
Answer: The main idea is that a sensor can provide early warning of overheating. The supporting detail is that the sensor measured temperature continuously and warned the operator.

2. Summarising a passage

A summary is a shorter form of a passage that retains its essential meaning. It should be objective, coherent, and written in the learner’s own words. A summary is not a collection of copied sentences and not a personal opinion.

Summary procedure

  1. Read for overall meaning.
  2. Underline the central idea and essential points.
  3. Remove examples, repetition, minor descriptions, and personal comments.
  4. Group related points and arrange them logically.
  5. Write a concise paragraph and check grammar.

Quality checklist

  • Meaning is preserved.
  • Important cause-and-effect links remain.
  • Pronouns and tense are consistent.
  • No new claim is introduced.
  • Length is appropriate to the task.
Given: A passage explains that regular maintenance prevents unexpected machine stoppage by identifying wear early, reducing repair cost, and improving safety.
Required: Summarise in one sentence.
Calculation/response: Regular maintenance detects wear early, reduces repair cost, prevents unplanned stoppage, and improves safety.
Answer: The summary keeps the main cause and the four linked benefits without copying the original sentence.

3. Word formation — prefixes, suffixes, synonyms, antonyms, and homonyms

Word formation helps a learner infer meaning and select accurate vocabulary. A prefix is added before a base word, while a suffix is added after it. Synonyms have similar meanings, antonyms show contrast, and homonyms have the same spelling or pronunciation but different meanings.

Word-building and meaning relationships
FeatureExampleLearning point
Prefixun + safe → unsafeThe prefix changes or reverses meaning.
Suffixdevelop + ment → developmentThe suffix can change word class.
Synonymaccurate / preciseChoose according to context, not just dictionary similarity.
Antonymformal / informalContrast must suit the same grammatical role.
Homonym“site” / “sight”Context determines meaning and spelling.

Engineering-context method

When an unfamiliar word appears in a technical passage, identify the base word, examine nearby prefixes or suffixes, use the sentence context, and test whether the inferred word fits the grammar. Do not assume that a familiar-looking word has the same meaning in every technical context.

Common mistake: “historic” and “historical” are related but not always interchangeable; “economic” and “economical” also serve different contexts.

4. Structure of a sentence, articles, auxiliaries, and concord

Sentence structure

A sentence normally expresses a complete thought. A useful basic pattern is Subject + Verb + Object/Complement. Longer sentences may add time, place, manner, condition, or reason. Clauses must be connected with suitable conjunctions and punctuation.

Sentence = Subject + Predicate; Predicate = Verb + Object/Complement + Modifiers

Articles

Use a before a singular countable noun beginning with a consonant sound, an before a singular countable noun beginning with a vowel sound, and the when the listener or reader can identify the specific noun. Some plural and uncountable nouns take no article when used generally.

Auxiliary verbs

Primary auxiliaries be, have, do support tense, questions, negatives, and passive construction. Modal auxiliaries such as can, may, must, should express ability, possibility, obligation, or advice. The main verb form must agree with the auxiliary.

Concord

Subject–verb agreement means that the verb form matches the subject in number and person: “The instrument works” but “The instruments work.” Intervening phrases must not distract the writer from the true subject.

Given: “The list of components are attached.”
Required: Correct the sentence.
Calculation: The subject is singular: “list,” not “components.”
Answer: “The list of components is attached.”
Malayalam support: Sentence-ൽ subject, verb, object എന്ന order മനസ്സിലാക്കുക. Subject singular ആണെങ്കിൽ verb-ും അതനുസരിച്ച് മാറണം: “The machine works,” “The machines work.”
5. Situational English

Situational English means choosing language according to the purpose, relationship, place, and level of formality. A workplace request should be clear and respectful; a conversation with a friend may be less formal. Effective communication includes greeting, turn-taking, clarification, confirmation, and closing.

Useful functions in real-life situations
FunctionSuitable expressionCommunication principle
RequestCould you please share the test result?Use a polite modal and specify the action.
ClarificationCould you explain the second step once more?Ask for the exact point of difficulty.
AgreementI agree with the proposed sequence because it reduces delay.Give a reason, not only “yes.”
DisagreementI understand the suggestion; however, the safety risk should be considered.Respect the person and focus on evidence.
ClosingThank you. I will send the updated file by 4 p.m.Confirm the next action and time.

Conversation pattern

Open the interaction, state the purpose, exchange relevant information, confirm understanding, and close politely. In technical situations, avoid vague phrases such as “do it quickly”; name the task, deadline, and expected output.

1. Comprehension and appreciation of the poem

Comprehending a poem involves identifying literal meaning, speaker, situation, theme, mood, and progression of ideas. Appreciation adds attention to imagery, sound, diction, repetition, comparison, symbol, and other poetic devices. The reader should distinguish what the poem directly says from an interpretation supported by textual clues.

Poetry-reading questions
QuestionWhat to look for
Who is speaking?Voice, point of view, and implied audience.
What is the situation?Place, time, event, or conflict.
What is the theme?The broader idea such as change, work, nature, or responsibility.
How is the feeling created?Word choice, rhythm, imagery, repetition, and contrast.

For an examination answer, introduce the central idea, explain two or three supporting images or devices, and end by stating the overall effect. Do not merely retell each line.

Malayalam support: Poem പഠിക്കുമ്പോൾ literal meaning മാത്രം നോക്കരുത്. Speaker, theme, mood, imagery, repetition എന്നിവ കണ്ടെത്തി text evidence ഉപയോഗിക്കുക.
2. Tense

Tense places an action or state in time. English commonly presents present, past, and future time through simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous patterns. Correct tense depends on meaning and time relationship, not on memorising labels alone.

Core tense patterns
PatternFormExampleUse
Present simplesubject + base verbThe pump operates at 50 Hz.Fact, routine, general truth.
Present continuousam/is/are + -ingThe technician is testing the circuit.Action in progress.
Past simplepast formThe team completed the test.Finished past event.
Present perfecthas/have + past participleThe team has completed the test.Past action connected to present.
Futurewill + base verbThe team will inspect the unit tomorrow.Future decision, prediction, or event.
Given: “The operator checks the gauge now.”
Required: Express the action as ongoing now and as completed yesterday.
Calculation: Ongoing: subject + is + verb-ing. Completed: subject + past verb + time marker.
Answer: “The operator is checking the gauge now.” / “The operator checked the gauge yesterday.”

3. Active and Passive Voice

In active voice, the subject performs the action: “The technician calibrated the instrument.” In passive voice, the receiver of the action becomes prominent: “The instrument was calibrated by the technician.” Technical writing often uses passive voice when the process or result is more important than the person.

Active: Subject + Verb + Object → Passive: Object + be + past participle + (by + Subject)
  1. Identify the object of the active sentence.
  2. Move it to subject position.
  3. Choose the correct form of be for the original tense.
  4. Use the past participle of the main verb.
  5. Add “by + agent” only when the agent is important.
Given: “The engineer prepared the report.”
Required: Passive voice.
Calculation: Object = the report; past simple passive = was + past participle.
Answer: “The report was prepared by the engineer.”
Common mistake: Do not change the tense while changing voice. “The team is testing the motor” becomes “The motor is being tested by the team,” not “was tested.”
Malayalam support: Active voice-ൽ subject action ചെയ്യുന്നു. Passive voice-ൽ action ലഭിക്കുന്ന object പ്രധാനമാകുന്നു. Tense മാറ്റാതെ be + past participle ഉപയോഗിക്കുക.
4. Describing people, places, things, and processes

Descriptive communication gives the reader enough accurate information to form a clear mental picture or understand a sequence. A description of a person may include role, appearance, behaviour, and relevant qualities. A place needs location, arrangement, function, and conditions. A thing needs identity, parts, material, dimensions where relevant, and use. A process needs ordered steps and cause-and-effect links.

Description planning frame
SubjectOrganising questions
PersonWho is the person? What role, qualities, actions, or evidence matter?
PlaceWhere is it? How is it arranged? What is its purpose?
ThingWhat is it? What are its parts, material, function, and limitations?
ProcessWhat is the input, sequence, control condition, output, and safety point?

Use specific nouns and measurable or observable details when available. Use sequence markers such as first, next, after that, finally for a process. Avoid unsupported praise or vague adjectives.

5. Reported Speech

Reported speech communicates what someone said without quoting the exact words. The writer must manage reporting verbs, pronoun changes, tense backshift where appropriate, time expressions, and question structure.

Common transformations
Direct speechReported speech
He said, “I am ready.”He said that he was ready.
She said, “I will send it tomorrow.”She said that she would send it the next day.
They asked, “Did you check the file?”They asked whether I had checked the file.
He said, “Close the valve.”He instructed me to close the valve.

Use said when no listener is named and told + object when a listener is named. In reported questions, use statement word order rather than question inversion.

Given: The supervisor said, “Submit the revised drawing by Friday.”
Required: Report the instruction.
Answer: The supervisor instructed the team to submit the revised drawing by Friday.

1. Phonetics — speech sounds

Phonetics studies how speech sounds are produced, transmitted, and heard. For practical communication, learners need awareness of the difference between spelling and pronunciation, vowel and consonant sounds, syllables, minimal differences, and the relationship between sound and intelligibility.

Pronunciation practice should be deliberate: listen to a model, identify the target sound, repeat slowly, record yourself, compare, and repeat in a sentence. The goal is intelligibility and confidence, not imitation of a particular accent.

Malayalam support: Spelling കണ്ടിട്ട് മാത്രം pronunciation തീരുമാനിക്കരുത്. Sound കേൾക്കുക, mouth position ശ്രദ്ധിക്കുക, word sentence-ൽ practice ചെയ്യുക.
2. Reading with stress, intonation, and pauses

Stress gives greater prominence to a syllable or word. Intonation is the movement of pitch across a phrase or sentence. Pauses divide meaning groups and help the listener process information. Good oral reading is therefore not simply reading every word with equal force.

Expressive reading controls
ControlPurposePractice cue
Word stressHighlights the important word.Underline the word that carries the contrast.
IntonationShows completion, continuation, question, or attitude.Mark rising or falling movement in the margin.
PauseSeparates sense groups.Use a slash at natural phrase boundaries.

Read once for meaning, mark the text, then read aloud while keeping the eyes slightly ahead of the voice. Avoid pausing after every word or breaking a phrase between a subject and its verb.

3. Capital letters and appropriate punctuation marks

Capitalisation and punctuation make written meaning visible. Use capitals at the beginning of a sentence and for proper nouns, official names, days, months, and the pronoun I. Punctuation marks show boundaries and relationships: a full stop closes a statement, a question mark closes a direct question, a comma separates suitable items or clauses, a colon introduces an explanation or list, and a semicolon joins closely related independent clauses.

4. Self-introduction

A self-introduction is a short, purposeful spoken text. In an academic or job-interview setting, include your name, programme or role, relevant education or skill, a genuine interest, and a forward-looking goal. The introduction should be adapted to the audience and should not become a memorised list of unrelated personal details.

Four-part structure

  1. Greeting and name.
  2. Current programme, role, or place.
  3. Relevant skill, project, interest, or strength with brief evidence.
  4. Purpose or goal, followed by a polite closing.

Illustrative model: “Good morning. I am Anu, a first-semester diploma student. I am developing my technical communication and basic programming skills through practical activities. I enjoy explaining procedures clearly, and I hope to contribute to a safe and efficient engineering team. Thank you.”

Delivery: Stand or sit upright, make natural eye contact, speak at a moderate pace, and use notes only as prompts. Do not claim skills you cannot explain.

5. Listening and comprehending

Listening comprehension requires active attention. Before listening, predict the topic. During listening, identify the main point, signpost words, numbers, names, contrast, cause, and conclusion. After listening, reconstruct the message and check whether the details support the main idea.

6. Note making

Note making converts a passage or talk into an organised structure. Start with a clear title, divide the content into main headings, use indented subpoints, and select only relevant information. Good notes are brief but meaningful enough to reconstruct the original idea.

Abbreviation rule: Shorten repeated words, but do not create abbreviations that only you can understand. Retain technical terms, units, names, and essential qualifiers.

1. Translation

Translation transfers meaning from a source language to a target language. A good translation is not a word-by-word substitution; it preserves meaning, function, tone, tense, and important technical relationships. First understand the sentence as a whole, then identify key terms, grammar, and context.

  1. Read the source sentence or paragraph completely.
  2. Identify subject, action, object, time, condition, and relationship.
  3. Mark technical terms that should remain standard English.
  4. Draft the target-language meaning naturally.
  5. Compare the result with the source and check omissions, additions, tense, and tone.
Translation quality checks
CheckQuestion
MeaningIs the same idea communicated?
TerminologyAre technical terms accurate and consistent?
GrammarAre tense, agreement, voice, and sentence boundaries correct?
ContextDoes the expression suit the audience and situation?
Do not translate technical names mechanically. Preserve standard terms, symbols, units, and abbreviations unless the target-language context has an accepted equivalent.

2. Effective use of AI tools

AI writing assistants and translation tools can help generate alternatives, organise ideas, summarise text, or improve language. They do not replace comprehension, judgement, source checking, privacy protection, or responsibility for the final document.

Prompt and verification checklist
StageGood practiceRisk to control
DefineState audience, purpose, format, length, and source.Vague output or wrong level.
ProvideGive only permitted, relevant information.Privacy leakage or confidential data exposure.
CheckCompare facts, names, figures, grammar, and citations with the source.Hallucination or unsupported claim.

3. Professional communication

Professional communication is purposeful, audience-aware, accurate, concise, and respectful. It includes emails, letters, reports, and presentations. A technical document should allow the reader to understand what happened, what is required, who is responsible, and by when.

Email structure

  1. Specific subject line.
  2. Appropriate greeting.
  3. Purpose in the first paragraph.
  4. Relevant details in a logical order.
  5. Clear action or deadline.
  6. Professional closing, name, and contact details.
Given: A job advertisement requests an application by Friday.
Required: Opening of a professional email.
Answer:
Subject: Application for Junior Technician Position

Dear Sir/Madam,
I am writing to apply for the Junior Technician position advertised on your website. I have attached my résumé and the requested certificates for your consideration.

Letter and report principles

A formal letter uses sender/receiver details, date, subject, salutation, body, and closing. A short report normally includes title, purpose, method or context, observations/findings, conclusion, and recommendation where required. Use headings, numbered steps, tables, and exact units when they improve retrieval of information.

Presentation delivery

Plan a clear opening, three or four main points, transitions, and a conclusion. Use readable slides or notes, explain rather than read, manage time, speak audibly, and answer questions respectfully. The source activity asks for a two-minute presentation delivered with clarity and confidence.

Sentence

Subject + Verb + Object / Complement

Main idea + essential supporting points − repetition − examples − opinion

Keep meaning, remove non-essential material, and use your own wording.

Subject + greeting + purpose + details + action/deadline + closing

Exam preparation

Module-wise questions and answers

Module I — Prose

Define a summary. (1 mark)
A summary is a concise statement of the essential meaning of a longer passage written in the learner’s own words.
Explain two steps in listening comprehension. (3 marks)
First, preview the topic and predict the likely content. During listening, identify the main idea and record only important supporting details using keywords. After listening, organise the notes and check whether the details support the main idea.
Explain subject–verb agreement with examples. (5 marks)
Subject–verb agreement means that the verb matches the subject in number and person. A singular subject takes a singular verb: “The instrument works.” A plural subject takes a plural verb: “The instruments work.” Intervening phrases do not change the true subject: “The list of components is attached.” In questions and negatives, the auxiliary carries the agreement: “Does the machine operate?” and “The machines do not operate.”

Module II — Poetry

What is the difference between active and passive voice? (1 mark)
Active voice focuses on the doer of the action; passive voice focuses on the receiver or result of the action.
Change into passive voice: “The technician tested the motor.” (3 marks)
Object = the motor. Past simple passive = was + past participle. Answer: “The motor was tested by the technician.”
Explain how to appreciate a poem. (5 marks)
Begin with the literal situation and central theme. Identify the speaker and mood. Explain selected images, comparisons, sound patterns, repetition, or contrast, and show how they contribute to meaning. End with the overall effect of the poem. Interpretations should be supported by words or images from the poem.

Module III — Listening and Speaking

What is intonation? (1 mark)
Intonation is the movement of pitch across a phrase or sentence.
Write three features of effective note making. (3 marks)
Use a clear title, organise information under main headings and subpoints, and record keywords rather than copying every sentence.
Explain how to deliver an effective self-introduction. (5 marks)
Greet the audience and state your name. Mention your programme or role. Add one or two relevant skills, interests, or experiences with brief evidence. State a goal or purpose and close politely. Speak at a moderate pace, use clear pronunciation, maintain natural eye contact, and adapt the content to the situation.

Module IV — Writing

State one principle of responsible AI-tool use. (1 mark)
Verify AI-generated content against the source before using or submitting it.
List the main parts of a professional email. (3 marks)
A professional email normally contains a specific subject line, greeting, clear purpose, relevant details, requested action or deadline, and professional closing.
Explain the role of AI tools in professional communication. (5 marks)
AI tools can suggest wording, organise ideas, summarise permitted text, support translation, and help prepare content. The user must define the task, protect confidential information, check factual and technical accuracy, compare the output with the source, revise the language for audience and purpose, and remain accountable for the final document.

Common mistakes

  • Copying a passage instead of summarising it.
  • Using a fluent but inaccurate AI sentence without checking the source.
  • Changing the tense while transforming active voice into passive voice.
  • Writing every listening word and losing the main idea.
  • Using informal or vague expressions in a formal email.
  • Adding unsupported interpretation to a poem or technical passage.

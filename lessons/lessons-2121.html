Module 1: Plant Diversity

8 Hours

1.1 Classification Systems

Biological classification is the scientific procedure of arranging organisms into groups on the basis of their similarities and relationships.

  • Two Kingdom Classification: Proposed by Carolus Linnaeus (1735). He divided the living world into Kingdom Plantae and Kingdom Animalia.
  • Phylogenetic Classification: Proposed by August W. Eichler (1878). This system is based on evolutionary relationships and genetic similarities.

1.3 Genetics and Diversity

Diversity arises from changes in the genetic material (DNA).

  • Nucleus: The control center containing genetic material.
  • DNA & Genes: DNA is the hereditary material. Genes are segments of DNA that code for specific proteins.
  • Gene Action: The process by which a gene's information is used to synthesize a functional gene product (protein).

Module 2: Plant Taxonomy and Morphology

13 Hours

2.1 Morphology of Flowering Plants

Morphology deals with the external form and structure of plants.

  • Root Systems:
    • Tap root (Dicot)
    • Fibrous root (Monocot)
    • Adventitious root (Roots arising from parts other than radicle)
  • Leaves: Parts include Lamina, Petiole, and Stipules.
    • Simple leaf: Single undivided blade.
    • Compound leaf: Blade divided into leaflets.
    • Phyllotaxy: Pattern of arrangement of leaves on stem (Alternate, Opposite, Whorled).

2.2 Inflorescence and Flower

Inflorescence: The arrangement of flowers on the floral axis.

  • Racemose: Main axis continues to grow; flowers in acropetal succession.
  • Cymose: Main axis terminates in a flower; flowers in basipetal succession.

Flower Parts: Calyx (Sepals), Corolla (Petals), Androecium (Stamens), Gynoecium (Carpels).

2.3 Angiosperm Taxonomy

Binomial Nomenclature: Scientific naming using two words (Genus and Species). Example: Tectona grandis (Teak).

Key Families for Wood Technology:

  1. Verbenaceae: Example: Tectona grandis (Teak) - Essential for high-quality timber.
  2. Fabaceae: Example: Acacia - Used for furniture and tannin.
  3. Myrtaceae: Example: Eucalyptus - Primary source for paper pulp.
  4. Euphorbiaceae: Example: Hevea brasiliensis (Rubber).

Module 3: Plant Anatomy and Tissues

12 Hours

3.1 Plant Cell Ultrastructure

The cell wall is the most critical part for wood technology. It consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin.

  • Pits: Thin areas in the cell wall where secondary wall is not deposited, allowing transport.
  • Plasmodesmata: Cytoplasmic channels between adjacent cells.

3.2 Plant Tissues

Meristematic Tissues: Actively dividing cells. Found at Root apex and Shoot apex.

Permanent Tissues:

  • Simple: Parenchyma, Collenchyma, Sclerenchyma.
  • Complex: Xylem (water transport) and Phloem (food transport).

3.3 Vascular Bundles

Arrangement of Xylem and Phloem.

  • Conjoint: Xylem and Phloem on the same radius.
  • Collateral: Phloem outside, Xylem inside.
  • Open: Cambium present (allows secondary growth).
  • Closed: Cambium absent (no secondary growth).

Module 4: Wood Anatomy

10 Hours

4.1 Primary and Secondary Structure

Primary growth increases length, while secondary growth increases girth (thickness).

Secondary Growth in Dicot Stem: 1. Formation of Cambial Ring. 2. Activity of Cambium producing Secondary Xylem (Wood) and Secondary Phloem. 3. Formation of Periderm (Cork).

4.2 Wood Characteristics

FeatureDescription
Growth RingsConcentric layers of wood produced in one year (Spring wood + Autumn wood).
HeartwoodCentral, dark, non-functional, durable part of wood (contains resins/tannins).
SapwoodOuter, light-colored, functional part for water transport.
TylosesBalloon-like outgrowths of parenchyma into xylem vessels; block water flow.

Module-wise Practice Questions

Q: Differentiate between Heartwood and Sapwood. (5 Marks)
Heartwood: Central part, dark color, physiologically inactive, highly durable due to deposition of resins/tannins, resistant to pests.
Sapwood: Peripheral part, light color, physiologically active (transports water), less durable, susceptible to pest attacks.
Q: What are Tyloses and why are they important in wood technology? (3 Marks)
Tyloses are balloon-like extensions of xylem parenchyma cells into the lumen of vessels. They block the vessels, making the wood impermeable. This is important for making wood resistant to decay and for certain industrial uses like barrel making.
Q: Explain the Binomial Nomenclature with an example. (2 Marks)
It is the system of providing a scientific name with two components: Genus and Species. Example: Mangifera indica (Mango).

Glossary

Autotrophic
Organisms that produce their own food.
Dendrochronology
Science of dating based on tree rings.
Lignin
Complex organic polymer that makes wood rigid.
Thallus
Plant body not differentiated into roots, stem, and leaves.

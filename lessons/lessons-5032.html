Module 1 · CO1 · 14 Hours

Electric Power Generating Systems

Generation is the first stage of the power system. This chapter covers energy sources, commercial and non-commercial generation, hydroelectric and thermal stations, site selection, hydel energy calculation, grid connection, interconnected grids, distributed generation and microgrids.

1.1 Energy sources and generation classification

Electrical energy is produced by converting another form of energy into mechanical rotation and then into electrical energy using an alternator. Energy sources may be classified as conventional and non-conventional, renewable and non-renewable, and commercial and non-commercial. Conventional sources include coal, diesel, gas, nuclear fuel and large hydro. Renewable sources include solar, wind, small hydro and biomass.

A generating scheme must be selected by checking fuel availability, cost, reliability, environmental impact, starting time and suitability for base load or peak load operation.

Malayalam: Electricity നേരിട്ട് പ്രകൃതിയിൽ നിന്ന് കിട്ടുന്നില്ല. Water, coal, gas, diesel, nuclear heat, solar/wind source എന്നിവ ഉപയോഗിച്ച് alternator rotate ചെയ്താണ് electricity ഉണ്ടാക്കുന്നത്.

1.2 Commercial and non-commercial generation

Commercial generation supplies electricity to consumers through utility networks and revenue is collected through tariffs. Non-commercial generation is mainly self-use generation, such as a factory diesel generator used during power failure. Commercial plants need metering, scheduling, grid connection and economic operation. Non-commercial sources are planned mainly for reliability, emergency support and local demand.

Industrial note: A factory may use grid supply as main source and diesel generator as standby source. This is generation planning at a small industrial level.

1.3 Hydroelectric power plant

A hydroelectric power plant uses the potential energy of stored water. Water from the reservoir flows through a penstock and strikes the turbine runner. The turbine shaft drives an alternator to generate electricity. Hydel stations have low running cost, quick starting, high efficiency and no fuel transport problem. Their limitations are dependence on rainfall, high initial civil cost and need for suitable site.

Hydel station: reservoir → penstock → turbine → alternator → electrical output.
Hydel formula: P = 9.81 Q H η kW, where Q is discharge in m³/s, H is head in m and η is overall efficiency.

1.4 Thermal generating stations

Thermal plants convert heat energy into electrical energy. Coal thermal plants burn coal in a boiler and use steam turbine alternator sets. Gas turbine stations burn fuel gas and are useful for quick starting and peak loads. Diesel stations are suitable for small capacity and standby supply. Nuclear stations use fission heat to produce steam and are suitable for large base-load generation with strict safety systems.

PlantMain energy conversionStrengthLimitation
HydroWater potential → turbineLow running costSite and rainfall dependent
Coal thermalCoal heat → steam turbineLarge base-load plantPollution and ash handling
GasGas combustion → turbineQuick startFuel cost
DieselDiesel engine → alternatorStandby useHigh running cost
NuclearFission heat → steam turbineHigh energy densitySafety and waste issues

1.5 Site selection, grid and microgrid

Site selection affects the long-term economy of generation. Hydro sites need water availability, head, reservoir possibility and stable geology. Thermal sites need fuel supply, transport, cooling water, ash disposal, land and grid access. Nuclear sites need safety distance, cooling water and strong regulatory control.

Interconnected grids connect different power stations and load centers. This improves reliability, permits power exchange, reduces reserve capacity and supports economic load sharing. Distributed generation places generation near loads. A microgrid is a local electrical network that can operate connected to the main grid or in islanded mode during grid failure.

Module 2 · CO2 · 15 Hours

Load Curve, Cost of Generation, Power Factor and Tariffs

This chapter links power station operation with economics. It covers variable load, load curves, demand terms, annual units, generation costs, power factor correction and tariff schemes.

2.1 Variable load and load curve

Power demand is not constant. Domestic load increases in morning and evening, industrial load may be high during working hours, and commercial load depends on business timing. A load curve shows demand against time. The area under the curve gives energy. It helps decide base-load plant, peak-load plant, standby capacity, maintenance schedule and operating economy.

Load curve is used for generation planning and energy calculation.

Malayalam: Load curve നോക്കി ഏത് സമയത്ത് എത്ര demand ഉണ്ടെന്ന് മനസ്സിലാക്കാം. Curve-ന്റെ area energy ആണ്.

2.2 Demand terms

Connected load is the sum of rated power of all connected equipment. Maximum demand is the highest demand during a period. Average demand is total units divided by time. Load factor is the ratio of average demand to maximum demand. Diversity factor is the ratio of sum of individual maximum demands to system maximum demand.

Important: Load factor = Average demand / Maximum demand. Annual units = Average demand × 8760. Diversity factor = Sum of individual maximum demands / System maximum demand.

2.3 Generation cost

Fixed cost remains even when no energy is generated. It includes interest, depreciation, permanent salary and land/building cost. Semi-fixed cost depends partly on plant capacity and demand. Running cost depends on actual generation and includes fuel, lubricants, water treatment and consumables. Understanding cost is necessary to design tariffs.

CostMeaningExample
FixedIndependent of generated unitsInterest, depreciation
Semi-fixedPartly capacity/demand relatedMaintenance, standby equipment
RunningDepends on actual generationFuel and operating consumables

2.4 Power factor correction

Power factor is the cosine of phase angle between voltage and current. Low power factor increases current for the same real power. This increases copper loss, voltage drop, conductor size and kVA rating. It may also attract penalty. Power factor can be improved using static capacitors, synchronous condensers and phase advancers.

Static capacitor supplies leading kVAr and reduces lagging reactive demand.
Capacitor kVAr: Qc = P(tanφ1 − tanφ2).

2.5 Tariff schemes

Tariff is the rate structure for charging electrical energy. It must recover cost, be fair, simple and encourage efficient use. Simple tariff uses one rate per unit. Flat rate tariff uses different rates for consumer categories. Block rate tariff changes rate with consumption block. Two-part tariff includes fixed charge and energy charge. Maximum demand tariff charges demand plus energy. Power factor tariff gives penalty or incentive according to PF. ABT and TOD tariffs help grid discipline and demand management.

Module 3 · CO3 · 15 Hours

Transmission Systems, Overhead Lines and Sag

This chapter covers primary and secondary transmission, AC/DC transmission, overhead line components, conductor properties, line losses, voltage regulation, transmission efficiency, economical voltage, sag, line phenomena, EHV, HVDC and FACTS.

3.1 Transmission schemes

Transmission carries bulk power from generating stations to receiving stations. Primary transmission is high-voltage transfer from generator station to main receiving station. Secondary transmission transfers power from receiving station to distribution substations. AC transmission is common because transformers easily change voltage levels. DC transmission is useful for long-distance and special interconnections but requires converters.

3.2 Overhead line components

Overhead lines include conductors, supports, cross-arms, insulators, clamps, earth wires, stay sets and protective accessories. Conductors should have high conductivity, adequate tensile strength, low weight, corrosion resistance and low cost. Important performance terms are line losses, voltage regulation, transmission efficiency and economical transmission voltage.

3.3 Sag and tension

Sag is the vertical distance between the lowest point of an overhead conductor and the straight line joining supports. Correct sag ensures safe ground clearance and prevents excessive mechanical tension. Temperature rise increases conductor length and sag. Wind and ice loading can increase mechanical stress.

Sag for supports at equal level: y = wL² / 8T.
Sag equation: y = wL² / 8T for equal supports, where w is weight per unit length, L is span and T is horizontal tension.

Malayalam: Sag കുറവായാൽ tension കൂടുതലാകും; sag കൂടുതലായാൽ ground clearance കുറയും. അതിനാൽ calculation safety-ക്ക് പ്രധാനമാണ്.

3.4 Transmission line phenomena

Transposition balances phase parameters by changing conductor positions. Skin effect increases effective resistance because AC current crowds near the conductor surface. Proximity effect is current redistribution due to nearby conductors. Ferranti effect causes receiving-end voltage to become higher at light load in long lines. Corona is ionization of air around high-voltage conductors.

3.5 EHV, HVDC and FACTS

Extra High Voltage transmission reduces current and losses for bulk power transfer. HVDC is useful for very long distance, submarine links and asynchronous grid interconnection. FACTS devices use power electronics to control line parameters and improve stability, voltage profile and power transfer capability.

Module 4 · CO4 · 14 Hours

Distribution Systems, Insulators, Cables and Substations

This chapter explains feeders, distributors, service mains, distribution schemes, DC and AC distribution, ideal distribution requirements, insulators, string efficiency, underground cables, cable laying, grading and substations.

4.1 Distribution system components

A feeder carries power from a substation to a distribution area. A distributor is the conductor from which consumer tappings are taken. Service mains connect the distributor to consumer premises. Feeder design mainly considers current capacity, while distributor design also strongly considers voltage drop.

Malayalam: Feeder supply area-ലേക്ക് power കൊണ്ടുപോകുന്നു. Distributor-ൽ നിന്നാണ് consumers-ലേക്ക് tapping. Service mains ആണ് consumer building-ലേക്ക് പോകുന്ന final connection.

4.2 Distribution schemes

Radial distribution has one supply path and is simple and cheap, but reliability is poor. Ring main distribution forms a loop and improves supply continuity. Interconnected distribution uses multiple sources or feeders and gives better reliability and load sharing but needs more complex protection.

SchemeAdvantageLimitation
RadialSimple, low costPoor reliability
Ring mainBetter continuityHigher cost
InterconnectedHigh reliabilityComplex protection

4.3 Insulators and string efficiency

Line insulators support conductors and electrically isolate them from supports. They must have high dielectric strength, high mechanical strength, low porosity, weather resistance and high insulation resistance. Types include pin, suspension, strain and shackle insulators. Insulator failure may be due to puncture, flashover, cracking, pollution, moisture or mechanical stress.

String efficiency: Voltage across whole string / (number of discs × voltage across disc nearest conductor) × 100%.

4.4 Underground cables

Underground cables are used where overhead lines are not suitable. Basic construction includes conductor, insulation, metallic sheath, bedding, armouring and serving. Laying methods include direct laying, draw-in system and solid system. Grading reduces dielectric stress and improves cable life.

LayerPurpose
ConductorCarries current
InsulationPrevents leakage and short circuit
SheathMoisture protection
ArmouringMechanical protection
ServingOuter protection

4.5 Substations

A substation changes voltage level, switches circuits, protects equipment, measures electrical quantities and controls power flow. Main components include transformer, busbar, circuit breaker, isolator, lightning arrester, CT, PT, relays and earthing system.

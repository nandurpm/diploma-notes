Module I

28 Hours (12L + 16P) CO1 Apply / Remember / Understand

Apply basic knowledge of the phonographic system, including consonants, vowels, joined strokes, and grammalogues.

The Phonographic System & Consonants

Phonography is a writing system based on the sounds of spoken language rather than traditional English spelling. It uses simple geometric strokes (straight lines, curves, circles, and hooks) to represent consonant and vowel sounds, enabling writing speeds of 80–120+ words per minute (WPM).

Consonant Strokes: Consonants are represented by light and heavy strokes of the same shape to distinguish voiceless and voiced sounds (e.g., light 'p' vs. heavy 'b', light 't' vs. heavy 'd', light 'k' vs. heavy 'g').

Malayalam support: Phonography എന്നാൽ ശബ്ദങ്ങളെ (sounds) അടിസ്ഥാനമാക്കിയുള്ള എഴുത്തുരീതിയാണ്. സാധാരണ ഇംഗ്ലീഷ് അക്ഷരങ്ങൾക്ക് പകരം ശബ്ദങ്ങൾക്ക് അനുയോജ്യമായ ലളിതമായ ചിഹ്നങ്ങൾ (strokes, circles, hooks) ഉപയോഗിക്കുന്നു. ഉദാഹരണത്തിന്, 'p', 'b' എന്നിവയ്ക്ക് ഒരേ ആകൃതിയിലുള്ള വരകൾ തന്നെ ഉപയോഗിക്കുന്നു, എന്നാൽ 'p' യ്ക്ക് നേർത്ത വരയും (light stroke) 'b' യ്ക്ക് കട്ടിയുള്ള വരയും (heavy stroke) ആയിരിക്കും. ഇത് എഴുത്തുവേഗത ഗണ്യമായി വർദ്ധിപ്പിക്കുന്നു.
Vowels and Grammalogues

Vowels: Represented by dots and dashes placed in three distinct positions relative to the consonant stroke: First place (beginning), Second place (middle), and Third place (end). This placement dictates the vowel sound without breaking the flow of the consonant outline.

Grammalogues: These are highly abbreviated, arbitrary symbols for the most common words in the English language (e.g., "the", "and", "of", "to", "is"). Memorizing grammalogues is critical for achieving high shorthand speeds, as they eliminate the need to write out common words phonetically.

Exam Tip: Be prepared to identify the position (1st, 2nd, or 3rd) of a given vowel sign in an exam. Practice writing grammalogues from memory until they become muscle memory.

Module II

28 Hours (12L + 16P) CO2 Apply / Understand

Make use of alternative signs for R & H, diphthongs, phraseography, and circles in shorthand.

Alternatives of R & H, and Diphthongs

R and H Alternatives: The 'R' sound can be written as an upward or downward stroke depending on the surrounding vowels and consonants to maintain joining fluency. Similarly, 'H' can be represented by light or heavy strokes or as a breath mark (aspirate) in specific contexts.

Diphthongs & Triphones: These are compound vowel sounds (e.g., 'I' as in "time", 'O' as in "go"). In shorthand, they are represented by specific joined strokes or enlarged vowel signs to indicate the glide from one vowel sound to another within a single syllable.

Phraseography and Circles

Phraseography: The art of joining two or more words into a single, continuous outline without lifting the pen. Good phraseograms are brief, legible, and follow the natural direction of writing. (e.g., "I am" or "will be" written as one connected shape).

Circle S and Z: A small circle represents the 's' or 'z' sound. It is written inside curves and on the right side of straight downstrokes. Large circles are used for 'st' or 'str' combinations, significantly reducing writing time for common suffixes and prefixes.

Module III

28 Hours (12L + 16P) CO3 Apply / Remember / Understand

Identify and apply loops and hooks to further abbreviate common consonant clusters.

Loops (ST and STR)

Loops are elongated, narrow ellipses used to represent the frequent combinations 'st' and 'str'.

  • ST Loop: About one-third the length of the straight stroke it is attached to.
  • STR Loop: About two-thirds the length of the straight stroke.
They are always written on the same side as the 's' circle, maintaining consistency in outline formation.

Initial and Final Hooks

Hooks are small curves added to the beginning or end of a stroke to add a consonant sound without adding a full stroke.

  • Initial Hooks: The 'L' hook (small hook on the inside of curves) and 'R' hook (larger hook on the inside of curves).
  • Final Hooks: The 'N' hook (added to the end of strokes to add an 'n' sound, e.g., "pen", "can") and the 'F/V' hook (a small tick at the end of a stroke).

Module IV

21 Hours (9L + 12P) CO4 Apply / Understand

Apply shun hooks, aspirates, and principles of essay writing in shorthand.

Shun Hooks and Aspirates

Shun Hook: A large, final hook used to represent the "-tion", "-sion", or "-cian" suffixes (e.g., "nation", "vision"). It is written on the same side as the 'n' hook but is significantly larger to encompass the "sh" + "n" sound.

Aspirates: The 'h' sound is often omitted in shorthand unless it is strongly pronounced. When required, it is represented by a light tick or a specific breath mark at the beginning of the outline (e.g., "hat", "he").

Essay Writing in Shorthand

Writing essays in shorthand requires a structured approach:

  1. Planning: Outline the main points in longhand or brief shorthand notes.
  2. Phrasing: Aggressively use phraseography for common transitional phrases ("on the other hand", "in conclusion").
  3. Transcription: Practice reading back the shorthand outline to ensure legibility before finalizing.

Malayalam support: Shorthand-ൽ പ്രബന്ധം (Essay) എഴുതുമ്പോൾ ആശയങ്ങൾ മുൻകൂട്ടി രൂപരേഖപ്പെടുത്തുകയും, സാധാരണ വാക്കുകൾക്ക് phraseography ധാരാളമായി ഉപയോഗിക്കുകയും വേണം. എഴുതിക്കഴിഞ്ഞാൽ അത് വായിക്കാൻ (transcribe) കഴിയുമോ എന്ന് ഉറപ്പുവരുത്തണം.

Formula Bank & Speed Calculation

Net Writing Speed (WPM)

WPM = (Total Words - Errors) / Time (in minutes)

Total Words: Number of words dictated.
Errors: Untranslated words, omissions, or miswrites.
Time: Duration of dictation in minutes.

Practice

Module-wise Questions with Answers

Module I

What is a phonographic system, and how does it differ from longhand?
A phonographic system is a method of writing based on the sounds of spoken language rather than traditional orthography (spelling). It differs from longhand by using simplified geometric strokes, circles, and hooks to represent sounds, allowing the writer to keep pace with spoken language (80-120+ WPM) without lifting the pen frequently.

Module II

Differentiate between Circle S and Stroke S.
Circle S is a small circle written attached to a stroke (inside curves, right side of downstrokes) to represent the 's' or 'z' sound without lifting the pen, making it faster. Stroke S is a separate, full-length stroke used only when a circle cannot be conveniently joined to the adjacent consonant (e.g., after certain hooks or loops).

Module III

Explain the use of initial and final hooks with examples.
Initial hooks are small curves at the beginning of a stroke to add 'L' or 'R' sounds (e.g., 'play' uses an L-hook on the 'p' stroke). Final hooks are added to the end of a stroke to add an 'N' or 'F/V' sound without a separate stroke (e.g., 'pen' uses an N-hook at the end of the 'p' stroke).

Module IV

What is a shun hook, and where is it applied?
A shun hook is a large final hook used to represent the "-tion", "-sion", or "-cian" suffixes. It is written on the same side of the stroke as the 'n' hook but is significantly larger to encompass the combined "sh" + "n" sound, as seen in words like "nation" or "vision".

Exam Prep

Quick Revision & Exam Checklist

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Writing the 's' circle on the wrong side of a straight downstroke (must be on the right).
  • Confusing the size of the ST loop (1/3 length) with the STR loop (2/3 length).
  • Lifting the pen when a valid phraseogram or hook combination exists.
  • Forgetting to transcribe shorthand outlines back to longhand with correct spelling and punctuation during lab exams.

Exam Day Checklist

  • [ ] Bring a sharp pencil/pen and a clean shorthand notebook.
  • [ ] Review grammalogues and circle placement rules one last time.
  • [ ] In Part C, choose the question where you can draw the clearest diagrams.
  • [ ] Manage time: Do not spend more than 15 minutes on any 3-mark Part B question.

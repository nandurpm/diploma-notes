Fundamentals of accounting

Syllabus point: Define accounting and classify various branches of accounting.

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying financial transactions, recording them, classifying them into groups, summarising them, and communicating the result to users. It is broader than bookkeeping, because it does not stop at writing entries; it also includes interpretation and reporting.

The syllabus asks for classification of branches of accounting. In practice, the important branches you should recognise are financial accounting, cost accounting, management accounting, auditing, taxation, and government or sector-specific accounting. The point is not to memorise names blindly, but to know why each branch exists.

Financial accounting looks outward to owners, creditors, banks, and other external users. That distinction matters later when you compare financial accounting with management-oriented reports.

Example: Example: If a business buys goods for cash, the transaction must be identified first, then recorded, then classified into purchase and cash accounts, and finally reflected in reports.
Common mistake: Common mistake: treating bookkeeping and accounting as the same thing. That is wrong. Bookkeeping is only one part of accounting.
Fundamentals of financial accounting

Syllabus point: State the advantages and limitations of financial accounting and explain important accounting terms.

Financial accounting is designed to record and present the financial performance and financial position of a business for a particular period. It shows profit or loss, assets, liabilities, and capital through formal statements.

Its advantages are practical: it provides a permanent record, helps determine profit, supports legal and tax compliance, gives information to outsiders, and helps compare one period with another when the same rules are followed.

Its limitations are equally important. It is historical, it may ignore non-monetary factors, it depends on accounting policies and estimates, and it cannot fully reflect the real market value of all resources. So the output is useful, but not perfect.

Important terms from this part include transaction, asset, liability, capital, expense, income, debtor, creditor, drawings, purchases, sales, loss, gain, and balance.

Common mistake: Common mistake: assuming the balance sheet shows the exact current market worth of the business. It usually does not.
Accounting concepts, principles, and conventions

Syllabus point: Describe the important accounting concepts, principles, and conventions.

This part is the discipline of accounting. The syllabus expects you to know the basic framework used in financial accounting.

Core concepts include business entity, money measurement, dual aspect, going concern, accounting period, cost concept, accrual, and matching. These concepts tell us what can be recorded, when it should be recorded, and how it should be presented.

Core principles and conventions include consistency, prudence or conservatism, materiality, full disclosure, and objectivity. These are practical rules that prevent arbitrary reporting.

The real point is simple: accounts must be comparable, reliable, and understandable. Without these rules, two accountants could report the same business in completely different ways and both could claim to be right.

Example: Example: Under the accrual concept, income earned during a period is recorded even if the cash is received later.

Bases of accounting and classification of accounts

Syllabus point: State the concept of debit and credit and the accounting equation; classify various accounts.

The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Capital. Every valid transaction keeps this equation balanced.

Debit and credit are not meaning 'increase' or 'decrease' in a universal sense. Their effect depends on the type of account. That is why beginners get confused if they learn debit and credit as random rules instead of structured logic.

Accounts are commonly classified into personal, real, and nominal accounts. Personal accounts relate to persons and organisations. Real accounts relate to assets and properties. Nominal accounts relate to expenses, losses, incomes, and gains.

Example: Example: If cash is received from a customer, cash account increases and customer account decreases or is settled, depending on the transaction structure.
Common mistake: Common mistake: trying to debit or credit without identifying the account class.
Rules of accounting

Syllabus point: Explain the British and American approaches to accounting rules.

The traditional British approach uses three rules: personal account — debit the receiver and credit the giver; real account — debit what comes in and credit what goes out; nominal account — debit all expenses and losses and credit all incomes and gains.

The American approach is based on account nature and balance direction, but the practical learning outcome in this syllabus is the same: choose the correct side using the nature of the account and the transaction.

In examination work, do not write vague answers. State the rule, then show how it applies to one example.

Malayalam support: British rule എന്നും American rule എന്നും പേരുകൾ വേറെയായാലും core idea same ആണ്: account nature നോക്കി debit/credit decide ചെയ്യുക.
Example: Example: When rent is paid, rent expense is debited because it is a nominal account and expenses are debited.
Common mistake: Common mistake: writing the rule without explaining what the words receiver, giver, in, out, income, and expense mean.
Journalising

Syllabus point: State the meaning of journal, journalising, and narration; prepare journals from different transactions.

The journal is the book of original entry. Transactions are entered there in date order. Each entry normally contains the date, particulars, ledger folio, debit amount, credit amount, and narration.

Journalising is the process of analysing a transaction, identifying the accounts involved, applying debit and credit rules, and writing the entry in standard format.

Narration is a brief explanation below the entry. It is not decoration. It tells the reader what the transaction was and why the entry was made.

The student must not only write entries by memory. The real skill is analysis. If you cannot analyse the transaction, you will write the wrong entry even if you know the format.

Malayalam support: Journal is chronological record. Narration is the small explanation line. അത് optional decoration അല്ല; transaction trace ചെയ്യാൻ അത്യാവശ്യമാണ്.
Example: Example: Goods purchased for cash will affect Purchase Account and Cash Account. The entry is written only after you decide the nature of both accounts.
Common mistake: Common mistake: writing the narration as a full essay or skipping it entirely. Keep it short, factual, and meaningful.

Syllabus point: Demonstrate the creation, selection, alteration, and deletion of a company.

The syllabus explicitly wants company-level operations in Tally before any voucher work. This is a practical foundation step. A user must know how to create the company, choose the correct company, alter company data when required, and delete incorrect company data.

Collection of accounts or ledgers

Syllabus point: State the meaning and types of a ledger.

A ledger is the book of final entry. It is the place where transactions are grouped account-wise after the journal records them in order.

The value of ledger is not in entry order, but in classification. It helps answer questions like how much is owed by a customer, how much cash remains, or how much has been spent on rent.

Types of ledger can be understood in practical terms as general ledger, subsidiary ledger, debtor ledger, and creditor ledger.

Malayalam support: Ledger account-wise record ആണ്. Journal chronological ആയിരിക്കുമ്പോൾ ledger ഒരു account-ന്റെ മുഴുവൻ history ഒറ്റ ഇടത്ത് കാണിക്കുന്നു.
Example: Example: All cash transactions related to the Cash Account are collected there, so the running balance can be seen immediately.
Common mistake: Common mistake: writing ledger entries in date order without grouping them under the proper account.
Preparation of ledger accounts, posting, and balancing

Syllabus point: Prepare ledger accounts, posting, and balancing.

Posting means transferring the debit and credit effects from the journal to the correct ledger accounts.

Balancing means finding the difference between total debits and total credits of an account and carrying the balance forward. If debit exceeds credit, the balance is usually debit balance; if credit exceeds debit, it is credit balance.

Ledger accuracy depends on correct posting, correct reference, and correct side placement. One wrong side can destroy the balance.

Malayalam support: Posting ചെയ്ത ശേഷം balance check ചെയ്യണം. Balance errors വരുന്നത് usually side mistake, omission, or wrong amount കൊണ്ടാണ്.
Example: Example: A ledger for Purchases will collect all purchase transactions, then the total can be compared with sales, cash, and other accounts as needed.
Common mistake: Common mistake: mixing balance c/d and balance b/d placement or forgetting to carry the balance forward.
Trial Balance

Syllabus point: State the meaning and purpose of preparing a trial balance.

A trial balance is a statement prepared from ledger balances to check arithmetical accuracy. It is not a final statement; it is a checking tool.

The purpose is to confirm that total debits equal total credits. It also helps in preparing final accounts and identifying many types of mistakes early.

However, an agreed trial balance does not prove the accounts are completely correct. Some errors, such as omission of entries in both places or compensating mistakes, may still exist.

Malayalam support: Trial balance എന്നത് books ശരിയാണോ എന്ന് check ചെയ്യുന്ന statement ആണ്. പക്ഷേ അത് perfect proof അല്ല.
Example: Example: If debit total and credit total match, the arithmetic posting may be correct, but a wrong account name can still exist.
Common mistake: Common mistake: treating trial balance as final proof of correctness.
Methods of preparing a trial balance

Syllabus point: Classify methods of preparing a trial balance: total method and balance method.

The total method lists total debit and credit of each ledger account.

The balance method lists only the closing balance of each account.

Both methods are valid. The choice depends on the information desired and the format used in class or software.

Malayalam support: Total method-ൽ totals കാണും. Balance method-ൽ closing balance മാത്രം കാണും. രണ്ടും ശരിയാണ്, പക്ഷേ purpose വേറെയാണ്.
Example: Example: A ledgers list with closing balances is shorter and easier for routine checking than a full totals list.
Common mistake: Common mistake: mixing the two methods in one statement without clear labels.
Advantages and limitations of trial balance

Syllabus point: State the advantages and limitations of the trial balance.

Advantages: it checks arithmetic accuracy, helps locate some errors, supports preparation of final accounts, and gives a clear summary of ledger balances.

Limitations: it cannot detect all errors, especially compensating errors, omission from both sides, wrong principle errors, and certain complete reversals that still balance numerically.

This is why the trial balance is an important stage, but not the final authority.

Malayalam support: Trial balance useful ആണ്, പക്ഷേ എല്ലാം detect ചെയ്യില്ല. അതുകൊണ്ട് blind trust ചെയ്യരുത്.
Example: Example: If two wrong postings cancel each other numerically, the trial balance may still tally.
Common mistake: Common mistake: ignoring the limitation part in theory answers.
Preparation of a trial balance from given ledger balances

Syllabus point: Prepare a trial balance from given ledger balances.

In exam work, you must list each ledger balance on the correct side, calculate totals, and see whether the statement agrees.

The practical skill is to know which balances go to debit and which go to credit. Asset and expense balances are generally debit; liability, capital, and income balances are generally credit.

If the totals disagree, the problem is usually in extraction or posting, not in the idea of the trial balance itself.

Malayalam support: Ledger balances ശരിയായ side-ൽ ഇടണം. Asset/expense സാധാരണ debit side; liability/capital/income credit side.
Example: Example: Cash balance appears on the debit side, while capital appears on the credit side.
Common mistake: Common mistake: adding all balances in one column without considering debit or credit sides.
Ledger creation and voucher entry in Tally

Syllabus point: Demonstrate the preparation of ledgers in Tally, alteration of ledgers, accounting vouchers from various transactions, alteration of vouchers, and display of trial balance.

The syllabus asks for practical Tally work that mirrors manual accounting. Students must create ledgers, alter them when needed, enter transactions using appropriate vouchers, alter voucher entries, and view trial balance.

This is not just software operation. It is accounting logic translated into software. If the logic is wrong, Tally will faithfully produce wrong output.

The ledger master must be named and grouped correctly, because voucher posting depends on that structure.

Malayalam support: Tally is not magic. Ledger setup wrong ആയാൽ voucher entry ശരിയായാലും output തെറ്റാം.
Example: Example: Creating Cash, Sales, Purchases, and Debtors ledgers before entering vouchers for a simple business.
Common mistake: Common mistake: entering vouchers without creating proper ledgers first.

Subsidiary journals and day books

Syllabus point: State the meaning, purpose, and advantages of subsidiary journals; describe various types.

Subsidiary journals are special-purpose books used to record transactions of similar nature separately.

The purpose is speed, control, easier posting, and cleaner records.

The syllabus expects understanding of purchase day book, sales day book, purchase returns book, sales returns book, bills receivable book, bills payable book, and journal proper.

Malayalam support: Same type transactions ഒരു book-ലേക്ക് കൂട്ടി എഴുതുന്നത് recording fast ആക്കും. അതാണ് subsidiary journals.
Example: Example: Credit purchases are not mixed with sales. They go into the purchase day book.
Common mistake: Common mistake: writing all transactions in one journal when the question clearly asks for a subsidiary book.
Purchase Day Book

Syllabus point: Prepare Purchase Day Book.

The purchase day book records credit purchases of goods. It usually does not include cash purchases because those are handled elsewhere.

It helps in tracking suppliers, purchase totals, and periodic posting to ledgers.

In a practical answer, the student should show date, supplier, particulars, invoice reference, and amount.

Malayalam support: Credit purchase മാത്രം purchase day book-ൽ. Cash purchase ഇവിടെ എഴുതില്ല.
Example: Example: Goods bought on credit from a supplier during the period are entered in the purchase day book.
Common mistake: Common mistake: including cash purchases in the purchase day book.
Purchase Returns Book

Syllabus point: Prepare Purchase Returns Book.

Purchase returns occur when purchased goods are sent back to the supplier due to defect, excess, mismatch, or damage.

The return is recorded separately so purchase totals are not distorted.

Debit note is the relevant document in the receiver-side context of the syllabus wording.

Malayalam support: Supplier-ന് goods തിരിച്ച് അയച്ചാൽ അത് purchase returns ആണ്. ഇത് normal purchase column-ിൽ ഇടരുത്.
Example: Example: Damaged goods sent back to the supplier are entered in the purchase returns book.

Sales Day Book

Syllabus point: Prepare Sales Day Book.

The sales day book records credit sales of goods.

Like purchase day book, it creates a neat record for later posting and control.

It excludes ordinary cash sales if the system being followed routes them through cash records or vouchers.

Sales Returns Book

Syllabus point: Prepare Sales Returns Book.

Sales returns happen when customers return goods sold to them on credit or cash, depending on the business practice.

This book keeps the sales records clean by separating returned goods from original sales.

Credit note is the relevant document in the sender-side context mentioned in the syllabus.

Credit note and debit note

Syllabus point: State the meaning of a credit note and a debit note.

A credit note is a document showing that the recipient of goods or services has been credited because of return, allowance, or adjustment.

A debit note shows that the sender or purchaser is debiting the other side for return or correction, depending on whose books are being maintained.

In exams, the safest answer is to tie the note to the accounting context and the reason for issue.

Example: Example: If a supplier receives returned goods, a debit note may support the supplier-side adjustment.

Bills Receivable Book and Bills Payable Book

Syllabus point: Describe the Bills Receivable Book and the Bills Payable Book.

Bills receivable records bills accepted by customers or others in favour of the business.

Bills payable records bills accepted by the business in favour of creditors or suppliers.

These books help with maturity control, cash planning, and later ledger posting.

Journal Proper

Syllabus point: State the meaning of Journal Proper.

Journal proper is used for entries that do not fit into special journals.

It is commonly used for opening entries, closing entries, transfer entries, adjustment entries, and other miscellaneous transactions.

Because the syllabus mentions it separately, you should be ready to explain why not every transaction belongs to a subsidiary book.

This is where the classification work becomes visible. The day books feed the ledgers, and the ledgers feed the trial balance.

In Tally, this structure is often hidden behind vouchers and reports, but the logic is the same.

Meaning and types of cash book

Syllabus point: State the meaning and types of cash book.

A cash book is a special record that handles cash and bank transactions. It works like a journal because transactions are entered by date, and it also works like a ledger because balances are maintained.

The syllabus expects the meaning and types: single-column cash book, two-column cash book, three-column cash book, petty cash book, and analytical petty cash book.

Because cash is sensitive, the cash book is one of the most important control records in practical accounting.

Example: Example: Every cash receipt and payment can be tracked quickly through a cash book balance.
Common mistake: Common mistake: calling cash book a normal ledger only. It has a dual nature.
Simple cash book

Syllabus point: Prepare Simple Cash Book.

The simple or single-column cash book records only cash receipts and cash payments.

It is used when the transaction set is simple and there is no need to split out discount or bank entries.

The balance is easy to compute, but the format becomes limited when more complexity appears.

Malayalam support: Cash മാത്രം use ചെയ്യുന്ന transactions-ന് simple cash book enough ആണ്.
Example: Example: Cash sales and cash purchases can be entered in a simple cash book format if the exercise demands it.
Common mistake: Common mistake: putting bank transactions into the single-column format without adjusting the structure.
Two-column and three-column cash book

Syllabus point: Prepare Two-column Cash Book with discount column; prepare Three-column Cash Book with cash, bank, and discount.

The two-column cash book includes cash and discount. The discount column improves clarity for cases where discount is given or received.

The three-column cash book adds the bank column. This is more realistic for businesses that handle both cash and banking transactions.

The syllabus also explicitly mentions enabling discount in F11 features in Tally for the two-column exercise.

Malayalam support: Two-column cash book-ൽ cash + discount. Three-column cash book-ൽ cash + bank + discount.
Example: Example: A customer pays cash and receives a discount, or a payment is made by bank with discount involved.
Common mistake: Common mistake: forgetting to separate discount from cash or bank amounts.
Petty Cash Book and Imprest System

Syllabus point: Prepare petty cash book under the imprest system and the analytical petty cash book.

Petty cash is used for small recurring expenses such as postage, stationery, conveyance, cleaning, and similar minor items.

The imprest system gives a fixed amount to the petty cashier. When expenses are spent, the amount is replenished back to the fixed level. This keeps control tight.

The analytical petty cash book classifies petty expenses into separate columns, making analysis easier.

Example: Example: A fixed petty cash float is used through the month and then reimbursed after the vouchers are checked.
Common mistake: Common mistake: recording tiny expenses in the main cash book and making the book messy.
Preparation of ledgers from cash books

Syllabus point: Prepare ledgers from cash books.

Even though a cash book records cash and bank transactions, some ledger linking is still needed in accounting systems.

Students should understand how cash book balances and postings relate to ledger control.

The important exam point is to show that the cash book is part of the ledger system, not separate from it.

Example: Example: Cash balance is reflected in the ledger-side understanding of the business position.
Common mistake: Common mistake: forgetting that the cash book also supports ledger control.
Tally cash and bank vouchers

Syllabus point: Demonstrate the recording of cash receipts and cash payments using Receipt Voucher (F6), Payment Voucher (F5), and Contra Voucher (F4); demonstrate two-column cash book and three-column cash book entries.

The syllabus names the voucher shortcuts directly, so you must know them accurately.

Receipt Voucher (F6) records money received. Payment Voucher (F5) records money paid. Contra Voucher (F4) is used for cash-to-bank or bank-to-cash type transfers.

For the two-column cash book work, discount handling must be enabled through the feature setting mentioned in the syllabus.

Example: Example: Money received from a customer by cash is entered in a receipt voucher; money paid for expenses is entered in a payment voucher.
Common mistake: Common mistake: using the wrong voucher just because the amount is cash.

Module-wise questions

Revision questions with direct answers

Module 1

What is accounting?

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating financial transactions.

How is financial accounting different from bookkeeping?

Bookkeeping records transactions, while financial accounting also classifies, summarises, and reports them.

Write the accounting equation.

Assets = Liabilities + Capital.

Name the three traditional classes of accounts.

Personal, real, and nominal accounts.

What is narration in a journal?

A short explanation of the transaction written below the journal entry.

Module 2

What is a ledger?

A ledger is the book of final entry where transactions are classified account-wise.

What is posting?

Posting is the transfer of journal entries to the relevant ledger accounts.

Why is trial balance prepared?

To check the arithmetic accuracy of ledger postings and support preparation of final accounts.

Name the two methods of trial balance preparation.

Total method and balance method.

Does a tallied trial balance guarantee correctness?

No. Some errors can still remain even if the totals agree.

Module 3

What is a subsidiary journal?

A special journal used to record similar transactions separately.

Which book records credit purchases?

Purchase Day Book.

Which book records credit sales?

Sales Day Book.

What is journal proper used for?

It is used for transactions not suited to the other subsidiary books.

What is a credit note?

A document used when a customer or recipient is credited because of a return or adjustment.

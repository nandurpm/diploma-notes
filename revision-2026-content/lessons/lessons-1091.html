Course Description

Polymers are crucial to modern life because they are lightweight, durable, and versatile. They are used in clothing, packaging, medical devices, vehicles, electronics, and many other products. The course introduces different types of polymers, their chemical structure, properties, and applications.

Students also prepare an album of polymer products available in the market and identify the polymeric material used in each product. This practical orientation makes the course useful for industry awareness and material identification.

Module 1: History of Polymers and Classification of Polymers

This module builds the foundation of polymer science. It starts with hydrocarbons and gradually moves into macromolecules, polymerization, molecular weight, and the major ways polymers are classified. A student who understands this module can read the rest of the course with much greater clarity because almost every later material is a polymer family or polymer product.

Why this module matters

Materials science often begins with chemistry. Polymers are long-chain molecules made from smaller chemical units, so one must understand monomers, bonds, chain architecture, and chain size. Once the molecular idea is clear, properties like elasticity, rigidity, transparency, and softening behavior become easier to predict.

History helps the learner understand how polymers moved from natural materials like rubber and cellulose to synthetic materials like polyethylene, nylon, and silicone. This shift changed packaging, transport, consumer goods, and engineering design.

History of Polymers

Early humans used natural polymers long before the science was formalized. Examples include tree gum, natural rubber, cellulose-based paper, silk, wool, and plant resins. These materials were not called polymers at first, but they already served as proof that long-chain substances could create useful materials.

Modern polymer science developed when chemists understood that many small molecules can link together to form giant molecules. This understanding allowed the design of synthetic materials with targeted properties rather than relying only on natural sources.

The development of polymerization techniques made it possible to create polymers for packaging, insulation, textiles, adhesives, tyres, medical devices, and engineering parts. Today polymer science is at the center of consumer and industrial material production.

Important point: the word polymer refers to a large molecule, while the basic units are monomers. The same polymer family can behave differently if its structure changes.

Learning outcome view

The student should be able to describe the history in simple chronological form:

  1. Natural materials used first.
  2. Scientific understanding of molecular structure.
  3. Development of synthetic polymers.
  4. Industrial scale production.
  5. Modern focus on recycling and biodegradability.

Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are compounds that contain only carbon and hydrogen. They are the simplest organic building blocks for many polymer-related chemicals. Polymerization chemistry often begins with hydrocarbon monomers because their carbon backbones are suitable for chain formation.

Hydrocarbons can be saturated or unsaturated. Saturated hydrocarbons have only single bonds between carbon atoms, while unsaturated hydrocarbons have one or more double or triple bonds. The presence of multiple bonds makes unsaturated hydrocarbons more reactive and useful as monomers.

Hydrocarbons may also be aliphatic or aromatic. Aliphatic hydrocarbons are open-chain or non-aromatic compounds. Aromatic hydrocarbons contain stable ring structures such as benzene and are important in the production of many plastics, resins, and additives.

Examples

  • Saturated: methane, ethane, propane.
  • Unsaturated: ethene, propene, ethyne.
  • Aliphatic: hexane, butene, pentyne.
  • Aromatic: benzene, toluene, xylene.

Applications

  • Fuel and petrochemical feedstock.
  • Raw material for polymer manufacture.
  • Solvents and intermediates.
  • Basic starting point for industrial organic synthesis.

Working principle: hydrocarbons with reactive bonds or ring systems can be modified into monomers or intermediate chemicals that later become polymers.

Saturated Hydrocarbons

Only single bonds are present. They are relatively less reactive than unsaturated hydrocarbons. Alkanes are the common saturated hydrocarbons.

Example structures: methane CH4, ethane C2H6, propane C3H8.

Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

Contain double or triple bonds. Their higher reactivity makes them suitable for addition polymerization and many industrial transformations.

Example structures: ethene C2H4, propene C3H6, ethyne C2H2.

Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Contain aromatic rings, usually benzene-based systems. Their stable ring structure is useful in resins, solvents, and comonomers.

Examples: benzene, toluene, styrene precursor chemistry.

Macromolecules and Micromolecules

Macromolecules are molecules with very high molecular weight and large size, usually made of many repeating structural units. Polymers and natural rubber are typical macromolecules. Micromolecules are small molecules such as NaCl or simple hydrocarbons.

The difference is not only academic. A macromolecule may show elasticity, toughness, viscosity, or fibre-forming ability, whereas a micromolecule behaves as a simple small molecule.

Malayalam: Macromolecule വളരെ വലിയ molecule ആണ്. Micromolecule ചെറുതാണ്. ഉദാഹരണത്തിന് NR വലിയ macromolecule ആണ്, NaCl ചെറിയ micromolecule ആണ്.

Definitions

  • Monomer: a small molecule that repeats to form polymer chain.
  • Polymer: a large chain molecule made of many monomers.
  • Oligomer: a short chain of few monomer units.
  • Repeating unit: the smallest structural fragment repeated along chain.

Examples: ethene → polyethylene, styrene → polystyrene, vinyl chloride → PVC, isoprene → natural rubber or IR.

Polymerization

Polymerization is the chemical reaction by which monomers combine into polymers. In addition polymerization, monomers add one after another without by-product formation. In condensation polymerization, small molecules such as water or methanol may be eliminated while the chain forms.

Degree of polymerization (DP) is the number of repeating units in a polymer molecule. Higher DP generally means higher molecular weight and often higher strength, although processing can become more difficult.

Relationship between DP and molecular weight

The molecular weight of a polymer can be approximated from the degree of polymerization using the repeating unit molecular weight.

\( Mw = DP \times Mr \)

Where \(Mw\) is polymer molecular weight, \(DP\) is degree of polymerization, and \(Mr\) is repeating unit molecular weight. This relation is essential for simple numerical problems in the syllabus.

Worked example 1: If \(DP = 100\) and \(Mr = 56\), then \(Mw = 5600\).

Worked example 2: If a polymer molecular weight is 56000 and repeating unit molecular weight is 112, then \(DP = 500\).

Important: in real polymers, molecular weight is often not exactly one value because chain length distribution may vary. The syllabus, however, expects simple approximate problems based on the repeating unit.

Natural, Synthetic, Derived

Natural polymers are obtained directly from nature. Synthetic polymers are manufactured by polymerization or related reactions. Derived polymers are modified forms of natural polymers.

Examples: natural rubber, PE, cellulose acetate. This classification helps students connect source, chemistry, and practical use.

Organic and Inorganic

Organic polymers mainly have carbon in the backbone. Inorganic polymers have backbones that include non-carbon atoms such as silicon, oxygen, or phosphorus.

Silicone rubber is a classic example of an inorganic polymer in the syllabus. It is important for sealants, insulation, and heat-resistant applications.

Homochain and Heterochain

Homochain polymers have a backbone of identical atoms, usually all carbon. Heterochain polymers contain heteroatoms like oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, or silicon in the main chain. This difference changes flexibility, polarity, thermal behavior, and chemical resistance.

Examples: polyethylene is homochain; nylon is heterochain because nitrogen and oxygen are part of the chain structure.

Homopolymer and Copolymer

A homopolymer uses a single monomer type. A copolymer uses two or more monomers. Copolymers can be random or alternating depending on the order of monomer arrangement.

Random copolymer: units arranged without strict order. Alternating copolymer: monomers alternate in a regular sequence. This structure affects flexibility, hardness, and processability.

Linear, Branched, Crosslinked

TypeStructureProperty trendExample
LinearLong straight chainsBetter packing, higher density oftenHDPE
BranchedMain chain with side branchesLower packing, lower density oftenLDPE

Crosslinking produces a network that improves dimensional stability and hardness but removes the ability to melt again. This is why thermosets behave differently from thermoplastics.

Simple numerical problems

Q. A polymer has repeating unit mass 84 and DP 200. Find the molecular weight.
A. \(84 \times 200 = 16800\).
Q. If polymer molecular weight is 25200 and DP is 300, find repeating unit mass.
A. \(25200 / 300 = 84\).

Expected Questions and Answers

Q. Why are unsaturated hydrocarbons important in polymer chemistry?
A. They contain reactive multiple bonds that can open and join into long chains.
Q. Give examples of homopolymers and copolymers.
A. Homopolymer: polyethylene. Copolymer: SBR.
Q. What is the main difference between linear and crosslinked polymers?
A. Linear polymers have separate chains, while crosslinked polymers have chemically joined networks.

Module 2: Natural Rubber

Natural rubber is one of the most important elastomers in industry. It comes from latex, undergoes preservation or concentration, and is finally converted into usable forms such as sheets and technically specified rubber. The module also explains the chemical basis of elasticity, the cis and trans structures, and the market forms commonly used in Kerala and elsewhere.

Origin of natural rubber

Natural rubber is obtained from the latex of rubber trees. Latex is a milky colloidal liquid found in certain plants. In the rubber tree, latex contains rubber particles dispersed in water along with proteins, resins, sugars, and other compounds.

The monomer unit of natural rubber is isoprene, and the polymer is polyisoprene. The naturally occurring form is mainly cis-1,4-polyisoprene, which gives excellent elasticity.

Malayalam: Natural rubber tree latex-ൽ നിന്നാണ് ലഭിക്കുന്നത്. Latex-ൽ water-ൽ dispersed rubber particles ഉണ്ട്. പ്രധാന monomer isoprene ആണ്.

Why natural rubber is elastic

Elasticity arises because long polymer chains are coiled in the relaxed state. When stretched, the chains align. When the force is removed, they return to a random coiled form. This reversible chain movement is the practical basis of rubbery behavior.

Strain-induced crystallization can also improve strength under tension. That is why natural rubber is useful in tyres and other high-demand products.

Structure, formula, molecular weight

Isoprene is 2-methyl-1,3-butadiene. The repeating unit in natural rubber is polyisoprene. A simplified structural representation is a long chain of cis-linked isoprene units.

Natural rubber has high molecular weight. The exact value varies by source, processing, and measurement method. Higher molecular weight usually means better mechanical performance but more difficult processing.

Properties

  • Excellent elasticity.
  • High resilience and recovery.
  • Good tensile strength after stretching.
  • Excellent abrasion resistance.
  • Poor resistance to heat, ozone, oxygen, and oil.

Latex forms

Field latex is the raw latex collected directly from tapping. Concentrated latex contains a higher percentage of rubber solids. Double centrifuged latex is a further refined high-concentration latex with improved uniformity.

Concentration may be done by centrifuging or creaming. Centrifuging separates lighter rubber particles from water using centrifugal force. Creaming increases the concentration by preventing particles from settling quickly and allowing the rubber phase to rise.

Latex preservation

Fresh latex can spoil or coagulate naturally. Preservation prevents premature coagulation and microbial degradation. In practical industry, preservatives maintain latex in stable form until processing.

Latex preservation is essential for transport, storage, and later product manufacture. Good preservation supports consistent product quality.

Cis NR and Trans NR

Cis natural rubber is the elastomeric form commonly used in tyres and flexible products. Trans natural rubber is related to gutta-percha type structure and is more rigid and less elastic.

Difference in cis/trans arrangement changes chain packing and flexibility. Cis chains are kinked and less able to pack tightly; trans chains are straighter and pack more closely.

Natural rubber industries

Latex industries and dry rubber industries process raw latex into stable market forms. In Kerala, the rubber economy is important, and the Rubber Board, RRII, Kottayam are relevant institutional names from the syllabus resources.

Job opportunities include latex collection, preservation, processing, quality control, product manufacturing, and testing.

Module 3: Synthetic Rubber

Synthetic rubbers are engineered elastomers created to meet industrial demands that natural rubber cannot always satisfy. Some are chosen for tyre applications, some for oil resistance, some for weather resistance, and some for heat stability or electrical insulation. This module includes the most important commercially available elastomers from the syllabus.

Raw material and meaning

Synthetic rubber is made from monomers obtained from petrochemical or related feedstocks. The exact monomer chemistry decides whether the final rubber is general-purpose or special-purpose.

The syllabus asks students to identify the raw material, structure, properties, and applications of IR, SBR, BR, IIR, EPDM, NBR, CR, CSM, and silicone rubber.

IR, SBR, BR, IIR

IR is isoprene rubber, synthetic material similar to natural rubber. It shows good elasticity and resilience. SBR is styrene-butadiene rubber, widely used because it gives good abrasion resistance and economy. BR is butadiene rubber, valued for resilience and low heat build-up. IIR is isobutylene-isoprene rubber, known for low gas permeability.

These four elastomers are very important in tyre and industrial rubber products. Their differences come from monomer choice and chain structure.

EPDM, NBR, CR, CSM, Silicone

EPDM offers excellent weather and ozone resistance. NBR offers oil and fuel resistance. CR offers weather, flame, and moderate oil resistance. CSM is valued for chemical and weather resistance. Silicone rubber offers outstanding thermal stability and remains flexible across a very wide temperature range.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages: designed performance, consistent quality, specific resistance to oil or weather, large-scale availability.

Limitations: some types are costlier, some are less eco-friendly, and many need compounding or fillers to achieve ideal properties.

Industrial uses

SBR and BR are major tyre elastomers. NBR is used in oil-resistant products. EPDM is used in weather seals and roofing. Silicone rubber is widely used in medical and electrical products because of thermal stability.

Module 4: Plastics and Fibres

This module covers thermoplastics, thermosets, common household uses of plastics, and fibre classification. It also introduces liquid resins, adhesives, and sealants. The student should understand both the chemistry and the everyday product context, since this is the most visible polymer topic in daily life.

Plastic classification

Plastics are broadly classified into thermoplastics and thermosets. Thermoplastics soften on heating and can be remoulded repeatedly. Thermosets form crosslinked structures after curing and cannot be remelted easily.

The difference between these two classes is crucial in product design, recycling, and processing selection. Thermoplastics are often used where reshaping and recycling are useful. Thermosets are selected for heat resistance and dimensional stability.

Malayalam support

Thermoplastics ചൂടാക്കിയാൽ soft ആകും, cooling-ൽ വീണ്ടും hard ആകും. Thermosets ഒരിക്കൽ set ആയാൽ വീണ്ടും melt ചെയ്യാൻ പറ്റില്ല. ഈ വ്യത്യാസം products തിരഞ്ഞെടുക്കുമ്പോൾ വളരെ പ്രധാനമാണ്.

Thermoplastics

Definition: polymers that soften on heating and harden on cooling repeatedly.

Examples: PE, PP, PVC, PS, PMMA, nylon, PET.

Five properties: light weight, easy processability, recyclable behavior, good chemical resistance in many grades, and lower cost for many applications.

Advantages: easy shaping, mass production, low waste, repairability in some processes.

Disadvantages: lower heat resistance than thermosets, possible creep, and softening at elevated temperature.

Thermosets

Definition: polymers that cure into a hard, crosslinked structure and do not soften again on reheating.

Examples: Bakelite, epoxy, phenolic resin, urea-formaldehyde, melamine-formaldehyde.

Five properties: high heat resistance, rigidity, dimensional stability, chemical resistance, and strong network structure.

Advantages: excellent thermal and mechanical stability. Disadvantages: not recyclable by melting and can be brittle.

Fibres

A fibre is a polymer material drawn into a long, thin form with high length-to-diameter ratio. Fibres are used in textiles, ropes, composites, reinforcement, and industrial products. The key requirement is good chain orientation, strength, and suitable drawing behavior.

Natural fibres: wool, silk, coir, arecanut, cotton, jute. Synthetic fibres: nylon, acrylic, PE fibre, PP fibre, carbon fibre, glass fibre.

Liquid resins, adhesives, and sealants

Liquid resins are polymeric or prepolymeric liquids that cure into solids, often by heat or reaction. Adhesives bond materials together by surface attraction and curing. Sealants fill gaps and stop leaks of air, water, or chemicals.

Examples: epoxy resin, polyester resin, rubber adhesive, silicone sealant. These products are widely used in construction, furniture, packaging, automotive, and electronics.

Question Bank

The question bank is module-wise and includes objective, very short, short answer, long answer, application, numerical, viva, and lab-style questions. Answers are kept concise but complete for revision.

Module 1 Question Bank
Objective: What is a polymer?
A polymer is a large molecule formed by repeating monomer units.
Very short: Define monomer.
A monomer is a small molecule that joins repeatedly to form a polymer.
Very short: What is an oligomer?
A short chain made of a few monomer units.
Short: Explain saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons.
Saturated hydrocarbons have only single bonds; unsaturated hydrocarbons contain double or triple bonds.
Short: What are aliphatic hydrocarbons?
Open-chain or non-aromatic hydrocarbons.
Short: What are aromatic hydrocarbons?
Ring-based hydrocarbons such as benzene derivatives.
Long: Explain macromolecules and micromolecules with examples.
Macromolecules are large molecules with high molecular weight like natural rubber; micromolecules are small molecules like NaCl.
Long: Classify polymers based on source, composition, and structure.
By source: natural, synthetic, derived. By composition: organic, inorganic. By structure: linear, branched, crosslinked.
Application: Why are alkenes important in polymer science?
Their reactive double bond makes them useful monomers for addition polymerization.
Numerical: A polymer has repeating unit mass 70 and DP 150. Find molecular weight.
10500.
Numerical: Polymer MW is 22400 and repeating unit mass is 112. Find DP.
200.
Viva: What is a homopolymer?
A polymer formed from only one type of monomer.
Viva: What is a copolymer?
A polymer formed from two or more different monomers.
Lab: Mention sample collection points for natural and synthetic polymers.
Natural polymers from plant or animal products; synthetic polymers from common packaging, household goods, and industrial samples.
Module 2 Question Bank
Objective: What is the monomer of natural rubber?
Isoprene.
Very short: Expand RSS.
Ribbed Smoked Sheet.
Very short: Expand ISNR.
Indian Standard Natural Rubber.
Short: State five properties of natural rubber.
Elasticity, resilience, tensile strength, abrasion resistance, and good recovery.
Short: What is field latex?
Fresh latex collected directly from the rubber tree.
Short: What is concentrated latex?
Latex with increased rubber content after removing some water.
Long: Explain preservation and coagulation of latex.
Preservation prevents spoilage and premature clotting, while coagulation converts latex into solid rubber using suitable coagulating conditions.
Long: Describe RSS, TSR, and ISNR.
RSS is smoked sheet rubber; TSR is technically specified rubber; ISNR is Indian standard natural rubber.
Application: Why is natural rubber important in tyres?
Because it offers elasticity, strength, and abrasion resistance.
Viva: What is the difference between cis and trans rubber?
Cis rubber is flexible and elastic, while trans rubber is more rigid and less elastic.
Lab: List products made of natural rubber.
Tyres, inner tubes, gloves, balloons, Hawai slippers, foam, sponges.
Module 3 Question Bank
Objective: What does SBR stand for?
Styrene-butadiene rubber.
Objective: Which rubber is oil resistant?
NBR.
Very short: Which rubber is known for weather resistance?
EPDM.
Very short: Which rubber is known for heat stability?
Silicone rubber.
Short: Mention five properties of BR.
High resilience, low heat build-up, wear resistance, good rebound, and blendability.
Short: Mention five properties of CR.
Weather resistance, flame resistance, moderate oil resistance, good durability, and good mechanical balance.
Long: Explain IR, SBR, BR, and IIR with properties and applications.
IR is NR-like synthetic rubber, SBR is abrasion resistant and economical, BR has high resilience and low heat build-up, and IIR has low gas permeability for inner tubes and seals.
Long: Explain EPDM, NBR, CR, CSM, and silicone rubber.
EPDM is weather resistant, NBR is oil resistant, CR is flame and weather resistant, CSM is chemically resistant, and silicone is thermally stable and flexible.
Application: Why is NBR used in gloves and seals?
Because it resists oils and fuels.
Application: Why is EPDM used in roofing and automotive seals?
Because it withstands ozone, weather, and outdoor exposure.
Viva: What is the main feature of silicone rubber?
Excellent performance across a wide temperature range.
Lab: Name product groups to collect for this module.
SBR, BR, IIR, EPDM, NBR, CR, CSM, and silicone products.
Module 4 Question Bank
Objective: Define thermoplastic.
A polymer that softens on heating and hardens on cooling repeatedly.
Objective: Define thermoset.
A polymer that forms an irreversible crosslinked network on curing.
Very short: Name five thermoplastics.
PE, PP, PVC, PS, nylon.
Very short: Name five thermosets.
Bakelite, epoxy, phenolic resin, urea-formaldehyde, melamine-formaldehyde.
Short: What is a fibre?
A long, thin, high-aspect-ratio polymer strand used in textiles or reinforcement.
Short: Give examples of natural fibres.
Wool, silk, coir, arecanut fibre, cotton.
Short: Give examples of synthetic fibres.
Nylon, acrylic, PE fibre, PP fibre, carbon fibre, glass fibre.
Long: Compare thermoplastics and thermosets.
Thermoplastics are remouldable and recyclable by melting; thermosets are rigid, crosslinked, and cannot be remelted.
Long: Explain properties and applications of fibres.
Fibres need strength, drawability, and orientation; they are used in clothing, ropes, composites, reinforcement, and technical textiles.
Application: Why are thermoplastics common in household items?
They are inexpensive, lightweight, and easy to shape into many forms.
Viva: What is a sealant?
A material used to fill gaps and prevent leakage.
Lab: Which samples are needed for the practical album?
Samples of thermoplastics, thermosets, natural fibres, and synthetic fibres.

Formula sheet

Higher DP generally means higher molecular weight and often better mechanical strength.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing monomer and repeating unit.
  • Thinking all plastics behave the same on heating.
  • Mixing up NR with IR.
  • Forgetting that silicone rubber is inorganic in backbone chemistry.
  • Ignoring product-property relationships.

Exam tips

Learn definitions first, then examples, then properties, then applications. For long answers, use a pattern: definition, structure, properties, examples, and applications. For practical questions, always mention sample name, class, three properties, and one or two applications.
